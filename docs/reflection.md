# English vs Spanish Vocabulary Recall Hypothesis Testing

## Background and Motivation

I grew up in a monolingual, English-speaking household and did not acquire my second language until early adulthood. In that time, I met my husband who also grew up in a monolingual household, but his native language is Spanish. Over the course of our relationship we have both acquired high levels of proficiency in the other's native language, but we typically level our communicative playing field with a preference for speaking a hybrid of Spanglish in our day-to-day interactions. This isn't usually a challenge for us, but occasionally we will get together with a group of friends at the idea of a game night has been pitched by someone. The problem is that depending who is planning the game night, one of us could always be at an inherent disadvantage in games requiring strong command of the language of play when nuance and careful attention to turns of phrase are keys to success. Up until now, the solution has been to default to games that do not have word-puzzles or complex story lines driving gameplay. This analysis looks to validate or refute the instinct to level the playing field by removing linguistic advantage from both parties by avoiding story-based game nights.

## Null and Alternative Hypothesis

H0: There is no difference in vocabulary recall in my native language, English, compared to my second language (L2), Spanish.
H1: Vocabulary recall is stronger in one of my spoken languages than the other.

## Description of Experimental Design

Two-tailed t-test will be used as I do not have a strong inclination or interest in asserting that one is stronger than the other. The literature I have consulted has suggested that the deeper cognitive engagement with the L2 could drive stronger L2 performance, but the study consulted compared an Indo-European L2 (English) with a Sino-Tibetan native tongue (Chinese, precise language unspecified) while both my native and L2 languages come from the Indo-European family.

## Power Analysis

effect size (d): 0.4 (based in literature analysis)
power: 0.8 (industry standard, accepting beta value of 0.2)
significance threshold (⍺): 0.05 (industry standard)
required sample size: 52 paired samples (same-person design, paired two-tailed t-test); 100 per group (200 total) if treated as independent groups. See docs/collection-plan.md.


## Data Collection and Cleaning Procedures 

### Data Collection - Leveraging Generative AI as a Research Partner 

To attempt to preserve integrity of the experiment as both the designer of the experiment and the test subject, I leveraged generative AI for the actual content of the experiment. Because LLM sessions and output are non-deterministic and reproducibility is paramount for test validation, I decided that the test itself must be shipped as an artifact that others could use. For this, I prompted Claude's Opus 5.5 to generate a CLI with inputs that would help me validate English and Spanish recall. This CLI code was shipped alongside the dataset and analysis. 

Additionally, choosing and preparing the vocabulary lists to test myself on was unnervingly reminiscent of our lectures on data leakage into models through improper handling of of the train/test/validate split, so I let a large language model do what it does best - generate language. The emoji input -> English/Spanish output was generated by Claude and a lot simpler than my own ideas for prompting output.

## Limitations
This experiment and data only represents my own cognitive and linguistic abilities and should not be used in an attempt to generalize to other individuals.
